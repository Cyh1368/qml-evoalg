# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns

- Mixed-CRZ/CRY entangling block with selective edges  
   • The current best program (“9-qubit RY-CRZ ansatz,” Gen 8) interleaves six CRZ gates on high-centrality links ([0,2], [0,3], [1,3], [2,4], [3,5], [5,7]) with six CRY gates on complementary pairs. This yielded a combined score of 0.60 (train 0.75/val 0.70/test 0.63) and stable training (std = 0.00).  
   • By contrast, CRZ-only designs (Gen 1: 0.58 combined, depth 47, 261 gates) or mixed CRZ/CZ layouts (Gen 15: 0.54) underperform.  

- RY→entangler→RZ “sandwich”  
   • All top-scoring circuits (current best Gen 8 and clustered Gen 13) use a single RY layer, then the entangling block, then a final RZ layer. This clean separation correlates with zero train/validation std and smooth convergence.  

- Moderate depth and gate count  
   • The Gen 8 seed uses depth 41 and 207 gates—matching the “sweet spot” in prior champions (Gen 6/Gen 10). Circuits deeper than ~47 (Gen 13 depth 59) do not improve combined score, and shallower parallel layouts (Gen 11 depth 39) dropped test accuracy.  

- Targeted entangler placement over full connectivity  
   • Focusing parameterized gates on ~12 strategically chosen edges maintains expressivity without over-parameterization. Full or dense entanglement (e.g. Gen 2’s ZZZ layers) adds noise and depth for little gain.

## Ineffective Approaches

- Over-parameterized CRZ-only layers  
   • Gen 1’s 12 CRZ gates (180 params, depth 47, 261 gates) scored only 0.58 and exhibited a generalization gap of 0.13, showing extra CRZ worsens overfitting.  

- Shared-parameter entanglers  
   • Gen 12 used shared CRZ parameters to reduce unique params but still had depth 47 and 231 gates, achieving only 0.55 combined and no convergence improvement—parameter sharing collapsed expressivity.  

- Excessive gate count without static trainers  
   • Designs exceeding ~230 gates (Gen 1: 261 gates; Gen 12: 231 gates; Gen 15: 231 gates) failed to surpass 0.58–0.55 combined scores, indicating raw gate count inflates noise.  

- Deep clustered entangling for generalization alone  
   • Gen 13’s hub-based CRZ clustering (150 params, depth 59) trimmed the gap to 0.09 but didn’t raise combined score beyond 0.60, suggesting depth reduction is also needed.

## Implementation Insights

- Distinct per-gate parameter naming  
   • The current best labels each CRZ/CRY as crz_0…crz_5 and cry_0…cry_5, and RZs as rz_post_0…8. Unique names avoid parameter collisions seen in earlier runs.  

- Gate ordering discipline  
   • Placing all nine RY rotations first, then the entire entangling block, then nine RZ rotations simplifies gradient flow and yields train/val std = 0.00.  

- Diverse two-qubit gate types  
   • Combining CRZ (phase entanglers) with CRY (amplitude entanglers) exploits complementary expressivity: Gen 8 (mixed) beats Gen 15 (CRZ + static CZ) by 0.06 combined.  

- Avoidance of three-qubit gates  
   • None of the top-scoring programs use ZZZ or CCRZ, confirming that added three-qubit interactions inflate depth without meaningful accuracy gains.

## Performance Analysis

- Top combined score plateau at ~0.60  
   • Both the current best (Gen 8) and clustered CRZ-Ry-RZ (Gen 13, 150 params) hit 0.60. However, Gen 8 achieves higher test accuracy (0.63 vs. 0.61) albeit with a slightly larger gap (0.12 vs. 0.09).  

- Parameter count vs. generalization trade-off  
   • Gen 8’s 180 params yield the highest test accuracy but a gap of 0.12. Circuits with ~144–150 params (Gen 10, Gen 13) hold the gap to ~0.09 while still scoring 0.60 combined.  

- Depth ≈41 and gates ≈207 as a performance sweet spot  
   • All best performers (Gen 6, Gen 8, Gen 10) center around depth 41 and 207 gates. Deviating deeper (>47) or compressing entanglers (parallel layouts at depth 39) lowers combined/test performance.  

- Generalization gap sensitive to entangler density  
   • Designs with dense or fully parameterized entanglement (Gen 1 gap 0.13, Gen 7 gap 0.11) see larger overfitting. Mixed but sparse connectivity in Gen 8 confines the gap to 0.12 while maximizing test accuracy.

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

Prune the two least‐central CRY gates on edges [4,8] (cry_3) and [6,7] (cry_5), freeing two depth slots, and insert a lightweight mid‐entangling RY sublayer on qubits [1,3,5,7,8] with unique parameters ry_mid_0…ry_mid_4. This preserves overall depth ≃41 and gate count, tests whether reallocating trainable capacity to single‐qubit rotations enhances generalization, and builds on the proven RY→entangler→RZ sandwich.  

2. Swap the CRY on wires [0,8] (cry_0) for a CRX gate (“crx_0”) in the exact same position. By introducing a third rotation axis into the two‐qubit entangler block, you maintain depth and gate count while probing whether added axis diversity further boosts expressivity and test accuracy.  

3. Remove the CRZ on the low‐impact link [0,7] (crz_2) and reassign that parameter slot to a new CRZ on the high‐centrality edge [1,2] named “crz_6.” This reallocation keeps total parameters at 180 and depth at 41 but focuses entangling power on a more influential topology, following the insight that strategic edge placement drives better performance.  

4. Group the six CRZ gates into two parallel sublayers—layer A: [0,2], [1,3], [2,4]; layer B: [0,3], [3,5], [0,7]—while leaving all CRY and single‐qubit rotations unchanged. This limited parallelization can shave ≈2 depth units without altering gate count, capitalizing on evidence that modest depth reductions on non‐critical gates retain the sweet‐spot expressivity.  

5. Convert the CRZ on [1,3] (crz_3) into an additional CRY gate (“cry_6”), shifting the two‐qubit ratio from 6×CRZ/6×CRY to 5×CRZ/7×CRY. Given that mixed‐axis entanglement outperforms CRZ‐only layouts, this slight bias toward amplitude rotations explores whether a CRY‐rich variant can lift the combined score above 0.60 while keeping depth and parameter count constant.
