# GLOBAL INSIGHTS SCRATCHPAD

The following are global insights about optimization approaches and their effectiveness:

## Successful Algorithmic Patterns  
- Strategic hybrid of trainable CRZ and static CZ entanglers  
  • Nine-Qubit Ry-CRZ-CZ-RZ Ansatz (Gen 10, combined 0.60) and the prior champion (Gen 6, 0.59) place six CRZ gates on high-centrality qubit pairs and reserve CZ for peripheral links. This preserves expressivity while capping parameter growth.  
- RY→entangling→RZ layer sandwich  
  • Both Gen 10 and Gen 6 use a single RY layer, then the mixed CRZ/CZ block, then a final RZ layer. This clear structure yields stable training (train std = 0.00) and smooth convergence, boosting validation accuracy to ≥ 0.66.  
- Mid-range parameter count (≈144–150) at moderate depth (≈41)  
  • Gen 10’s 150 parameters/depth 41 circuit outperforms larger (168–186 params) or smaller/deeper designs, hitting the “Goldilocks” zone for combined score (0.60) and test accuracy (0.61).  
- Sparse, targeted connectivity  
  • Focusing CRZ on six carefully chosen edges (e.g. [0,2],[0,3],[1,3],[2,4],[3,5],[5,7]) rather than full all-to-all or nearest-neighbor entangling achieves high generalization (gap ≈ 0.09) without over-parameterization.  

## Ineffective Approaches  
- Excessive depth/gate count with multi-qubit interactions  
  • Gen 2 (186 params, depth 88, 345 gates) only scores 0.56 despite adding three ZZZ layers, showing heavy three-qubit gates add noise/depth with marginal accuracy gain.  
- Over-parameterized CRZ-only layouts  
  • Gen 7’s 168 params/depth 47 “learnable_entangling_crz” yields a low combined score (0.54) and large gap (0.11), indicating extra CRZ gates beyond six key links exacerbate overfitting.  
- Fully parallel entangling to cut depth  
  • Gen 11 reduces depth to 39 via parallel CRZ/CZ layers but drops test accuracy to 0.55 (combined 0.56), revealing that compressing the entangling schedule can hurt expressivity.  
- Bloated rotation layers without static entanglers  
  • Gen 9 (180 params, depth 41) achieves high train accuracy (0.75) but only 0.61 test and a wide gap (0.14), illustrating that extra RY/RZ parameters alone accelerate overfitting.  

## Implementation Insights  
- Unique per-gate parameter naming  
  • Gen 10’s distinct crz_0…crz_6 and rz_post_0…rz_post_8 avoid unintended parameter sharing, unlike earlier failed attempts where reuse collapsed gradients.  
- Clear separation of concerns in gate ordering  
  • Implementing all single-qubit RY rotations first, followed by the entangling block, then RZ rotations simplifies the gradient landscape and stabilizes training.  
- Balanced static/trainable entangler mix  
  • Embedding static CZs on less critical edges (e.g. [0,8],[1,8],[2,6],[4,8],[6,7]) complements CRZs, preserving circuit connectivity while keeping parameter count in check.  
- Targeted connectivity design  
  • Gen 10’s choice of six CRZ edges corresponds to nodes with high network centrality, a pattern replicated in Gen 6, underlining the importance of strategic entangler placement over uniform graphs.  

## Performance Analysis  
- Optimal combined score aligns with 144–150 parameters  
  • Circuits in this range (Gen 6:0.59, Gen 10:0.60) outperform both smaller (Gen 11:144 params → 0.56) and larger designs (Gen 7:168→0.54, Gen 2:186→0.56).  
- Depth ≈41 as a sweet spot  
  • Gen 6, Gen 9, and Gen 10 all use depth 41 and achieve test accuracy ≈ 0.61, whereas shallower (depth 39, Gen 11) or deeper (depth 47–88, Gen 7/2) circuits underperform.  
- Gate count ≈207 correlates with peak performance  
  • The best programs cluster at ~207 gates; inflating to > 260 gates (Gen 7/2) or compressing entanglers (Gen 11’s 207 gates but parallel layout) yields lower combined scores.  
- Controlled generalization gap  
  • Gen 10 and Gen 6 maintain a gap of ~0.09, while over-parameterized designs (Gen 9 gap 0.14; Gen 7 gap 0.11) exhibit pronounced overfitting.

# META RECOMMENDATIONS

The following are actionable recommendations for the next program generations:

1. Replace the CRZ gate on wires [0, 7] with a trainable CRY gate (“cry_0”) keeping it in the same position.  
   Evidence shows axis diversity in two-qubit rotations can enrich expressivity (see previous successes with mixed CRZ/CZ); swapping CRZ→CRY on this high-centrality link tests whether cross-axis entanglement further improves convergence without changing depth or gate count.

2. Insert a lightweight mid-entangling RZ sublayer on qubits [1, 3, 5, 7] with unique parameters “rz_mid_0…rz_mid_3” (+4 params, +1 depth).  
   The proven RY→entangler→RZ sandwich yields stable training; adding a small extra RZ block in the middle extends that pattern, focusing extra phase flexibility on alternating qubits without blowing up depth or gate count.

3. Reorder the static CZ gates into two parallel layers to shave off ~2 depth: group CZ([1, 8]) with CZ([2, 6]) in one layer and CZ([0, 8]) with CZ([6, 7]) in another.  
   Prior attempts at limited parallelization preserved expressivity when applied only to non-critical static entanglers, helping reduce depth while maintaining the sweet-spot gate count (~207) and parameter regime (~150).

4. Remove the CRZ on the less‐central pair [0, 7] and reallocate it as a new CRZ on the high-centrality edge [1, 2] with parameter “crz_7” (same total params, same depth).  
   Champion designs focus trainable entanglement on top centrality links; shifting one CRZ from a lower-impact to a higher-impact edge tests whether that reallocation boosts generalization and combined score.

5. Replace the CRZ gate on wires [3, 5] with a trainable CRX gate (“crx_0”) in the same spot.  
   Introducing CRX entanglers on a central link diversifies rotation axes and builds on the static/trainable hybrid insight, probing whether cross-axis control rotations further enhance validation accuracy without altering circuit depth.
