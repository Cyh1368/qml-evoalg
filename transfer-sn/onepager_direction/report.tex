\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=white,colframe=rulec,boxrule=0.5pt,arc=2pt,left=6pt,right=6pt,top=4pt,bottom=4pt]
{\footnotesize\textbf{If you read nothing else.} Use a strong ensemble, run it 5 to 10 times with the
same settings, check the \emph{circuits} rather than the commentary, and confirm by actually applying
the transformation to the inputs. The model's own write-up of what it discovered carries no evidential
weight: the mid-tier ensemble claimed the symmetry in 5 runs out of 5 and built it in 1.}
\end{tcolorbox}

\vspace{3pt}
\begin{multicols}{2}

\shead{What to notice}
{\footnotesize
\textbf{The model's write-up tells you nothing about whether it found something.} Mid-tier ensembles
wrote confident symmetry notes in \emph{every} run, 5/5, and built the target structure in one. A
researcher reading only the patch notes would have concluded five times over that the symmetry was
found.\par
\textbf{A high score does not merely fail, it points the wrong way.} The two best-scoring weak runs
both had the wrong structure; the correct structure appeared at ranks 3 and 4. The natural move, take
the winner and study it, is the move that misleads you.\par
\textbf{Wild swings between identical reruns mean the instrument is not working.} Ten byte-identical
weak runs spanned 0.00 to 0.75, and one never improved on the starting circuit at all.}

\shead{What to do}
{\footnotesize
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Run it 5 times, ideally 10, same settings.} Once is not an experiment.
\item \textbf{Pull the actual circuit from each run and check whether the same structural pattern
recurs.} Does one shared parameter drive the same gate across all qubits? Not ``does the model mention
it'' but ``does the artifact contain it''. Ours: 3/3 strong, 1/5 mid, 2/10 weak. This is the test.
\item \textbf{Then test the behaviour directly.} If you think you found ``relabelling the qubits does
not matter'', relabel them and check the output is unchanged. No judgement call about which pattern to
look for. Trust this one most.
\item \textbf{Fix what counts as a hit before looking at results.} We chose ours knowing the answer:
fine for a study, not for real work.
\item \textbf{If only one model ever produces the structure, be suspicious.} One model found it 18/20
cold starts against 1/21 for the other two. That may be about the model, not your problem.
\end{enumerate}}

\shead{The one thing this cannot yet tell you}
{\footnotesize
If reruns \emph{disagree}, that means ``this resolved nothing'', not ``there is no symmetry''. The two
are indistinguishable from where you sit. Weak and mid gave inconsistent results on a problem that
genuinely does have the symmetry, purely because they were not good enough to find it. We know a
\emph{strong} ensemble correctly returns nothing when there is nothing (0/3 on a dataset built with no
symmetry). What weak and mid do in that situation is the experiment still pending.}

\shead{Evidence}
{\scriptsize
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
& weak & mid & frontier \\
\midrule

named any symmetry & 8.5\% & \textbf{81.2\%} & 40.8\% \\
named \emph{permutation} & 0\% & \textbf{0\%} & 30.6\% \\

built the $S_8$ orbit & 27.7\% & \textbf{0\%} & 83.3\% \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\par}
\vspace{1pt}
{\scriptsize Negative control (2026-08-18): three frontier runs on a dataset identical except that the
label rule was changed to destroy the symmetry. Best programs kept 16, 24 and 20 free parameters
against the seed's 24, with \textbf{zero} tied families, versus 4, 2 and 5 parameters and 3/3 on the
real task. Fisher $p=0.10$, pre-registered as suggestive. The null runs \emph{did} propose tied
circuits early; the fitness function rejected them.}

\shead{Correcting the framing}
{\footnotesize
\textbf{Not hallucination.} Matched claim to matched structure, mid's follow-through on the symmetry
\emph{it} named is 35/36 = 97\%. It named \emph{mirror} symmetry (a real symmetry of the circuit
layout, not of the task) in 75\% of proposals and \emph{permutation} symmetry in 0\%, and built mirror
faithfully. The defect is \emph{hypothesis lock-in}, not honesty, and that is the stronger claim.\par
\textbf{Verbal consistency across reruns fails as a rule.} Mid satisfies it 5/5 and is wrong in 4.
Only structural recurrence separates.\par
\textbf{Guideline, not benchmark.} weak vs mid is unresolvable ($d=0.19$, $\sim$63 runs/arm) and weak
outscores mid at a third the cost.}

\shead{Prior art, and the gap}
{\footnotesize
Stability selection (Meinshausen \& B\"uhlmann 2010) is the statistical ancestor but perturbs the
data, whereas here the randomness is LLM sampling. AlphaEvolve, TurboEvolve and QuantaAlpha report
rerun spread, always as an error bar and never as an inferential quantity. arXiv:2605.20086 is the
likeliest partial scoop and is known only from a search snippet; read it before drafting. Nothing
found treats rerun variance as a diagnostic for whether a discovery is \emph{real}, or validates such
a criterion against a known hidden symmetry \emph{plus} a matched negative control.}

\shead{Next, by value per dollar}
{\footnotesize
(1) Behavioural equivariance on the six best programs already extracted, $\sim$free, removes the
protocol's most attackable point. (2) Weak and mid null arms, $\sim$\$9, closes the gap above.
(3) Null replicates 3 $\to$ 10, $\sim$\$40, moves Fisher below 0.05. (4) A second symmetry class
(\texttt{zc-su2}), fingerprint pre-registered, defends against ``permutation invariance is
over-represented in pretraining''. Not yet scheduled.}
