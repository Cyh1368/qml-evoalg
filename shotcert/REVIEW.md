# Referee report: *Symmetry Spending Under a Quantum Shot Budget*

**Recommendation: Reject**

## Summary and assessment

The submission asks a worthwhile question: how should a frozen quantum predictor spend a fixed number of inference shots on symmetry-related circuits, and how can an independent calibration experiment certify the resulting finite-shot predictor? Its risk estimand includes the randomness of the predictor actually deployed. The authors also report negative findings about their first choice of concentration bound rather than presenting its apparent calibration frontier as a physical limit. These are strengths.

The manuscript nevertheless does not meet the novelty, rigor, or impact standard for a top-tier quantum computing journal. The implemented distribution-free bound is not the empirical-Bernstein bound it cites. The calibration budget omits most policy evaluations. The claimed switching-cost optimum is produced by charging more distinct circuits than exist. The numerical identity check is not the independent enumeration described in the abstract. Finally, the surviving theoretical results are elementary variance decompositions, with no demonstrated complexity or resource separation from the relevant baselines. These problems affect the central conclusions and require a new analysis and experiment, rather than a rebuttal confined to explanation.

## Major concerns

### 1. The claimed distribution-free certificate is invalid

Section 4 bounds a paired loss contrast, $D_i=(Z_a-Y_i)^2-(Z_{a_0}-Y_i)^2$, using an empirical-Bernstein inequality over the fixed menu. This is a reasonable generic starting point. Its implementation in [`certify.py`](certify.py), however, uses

$$
\ell=\log\!\left(\frac{4}{\delta n}\right)
$$

after the caller divides $\delta$ by the number of candidates. The Maurer–Pontil bound for a fixed variable in $[0,1]$ uses $\log(2/\delta)$; a simultaneous bound over $m$ candidates uses, for example, $\log(2m/\delta)$. There is no $1/n$ inside that logarithm. The implemented logarithm shrinks as the sample size grows and can become negative. In fact, the generated variance table contains a negative range term, which cannot be part of a valid one-sided confidence radius.

The range scaling is also wrong. Each squared loss is in $[0,4]$, but their difference $D_i$ is in $[-4,4]$, an interval of width **8**, not 4. The code sets `LOSS_RANGE = 4.0` and applies that value as the contrast's width in the range term. Its extra factor in the square-root term does not repair the incorrect logarithm or range term. For illustration, at $n=400$, $m=11$, and $\delta=0.05$, the code uses a logarithm of about $0.79$, whereas $\log(2m/\delta)$ is about $6.09$. The reported certified power and the $n$-versus-$S$ frontier therefore cannot be interpreted as results of a distribution-free certificate.

The Studentised alternative is appropriately described as lacking a finite-sample distribution-free guarantee. Its observed violation frequency in this collection of simulated tasks is useful diagnostic evidence, but it does not establish a $1-\delta$ certificate for a new task distribution. The paper should correct the bound, rerun the complete selection experiment, and report uncertainty around coverage and power using the *declared* risk threshold.

### 2. The calibration resource accounting omits policy evaluations

The paper's operational outcome is the resources needed to certify a useful policy. It labels the fixed-budget frontier $B=nS$, where $n$ is the number of independent labeled task instances and $S$ is the deployment shot count per policy. Yet [`calibration_losses()`](certify.py) runs **every** candidate and the raw baseline on **every** calibration instance, using independently sampled measurements for each policy. The actual calibration shot count is the sum of the shots used by all of those evaluations. It is approximately $nS$ multiplied by the number of policies, not $nS$.

The distinction between independent task instances and physical state preparations also matters. Repeated shots require repeated preparations, even when they share a classical input or Hamiltonian setting. Calling $n$ the number of preparations while drawing $S$ shots on each of many policies conflates these quantities. A valid resource frontier must separately state labeled instances, physical state copies/preparations, shots, circuit executions or switches, and any compilation cost. The menu changes with $S$, so its evaluation cost must be recomputed at each frontier point. Without this ledger, the primary cost comparison is not at a matched total budget.

### 3. The interior switching-cost optimum is generated by an impossible distinct-circuit count

The simulated orbit has two elements. At most two distinct transformed circuits can be compiled for any $k$. Nevertheless, [`Policy.circuits`](core.py) reports `min(k, orbit_size * 4)` for randomized draws, and the switching experiment in [`exp_control.py`](experiments/exp_control.py) charges `min(k, 4 * len(group.actions))`. Thus it can charge eight *distinct* circuits in a two-circuit experiment. That artificial increase yields the reported $k=2$ optimum when $c$ is large.

Under the manuscript's stated per-distinct-circuit cost, every $k\geq2$ has the same maximum circuit count, while the group-draw variance decreases with $k$. A cost for changing circuit settings during the shot sequence might produce a genuine interior tradeoff, but it is a different resource. The authors would need to specify a batching or interleaving schedule, count actual switches, and then optimize and verify that model. As written, the second main consequence in the abstract is unsupported.

### 4. The “brute-force enumeration” is not independent of the tested identity

The abstract and Table 1 describe a brute-force enumeration of the two-qubit risk that verifies the decomposition to machine precision. In [`core.py`](core.py), `exact_risk()` obtains estimator means and variances from `_estimator_moments()`; its docstring explicitly states that it does **not** enumerate shot outcomes and need not enumerate group draws. `components()` calculates the same constituent moments in another form. Agreement to $10^{-17}$ primarily checks algebraic consistency between two related code paths, not an independent enumeration of the stochastic predictor's outcomes.

The Monte Carlo checks provide a more independent, albeit less precise, comparison and deserve inclusion. For the claimed exact control, the authors should also enumerate the binomial outcomes and group-draw sequences for genuinely small $S$ and $k$, or use an independently implemented reference. The paper should distinguish that check from its closed-form moment calculations.

### 5. The physical action, target, and confirmatory threshold need repair

The Heisenberg task interprets $\Delta\mapsto-\Delta$ as a chain-mirror symmetry. A mirror of the full experiment also acts on the bond pattern, initial Néel state, and site-0 observable. The implemented open chain has alternating bonds, a fixed initial state, and a fixed-site readout. The manuscript does not derive an operator relation showing that flipping only $\Delta$ is the relevant symmetry of the full response or target. It may still be a useful paired-input transformation, but the paper's physical symmetry claim needs that derivation and an explicit treatment of the transformed preparation and measurement.

The frozen [`PROTOCOL.md`](docs/PROTOCOL.md) declares $\epsilon=0.02\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ for physical tasks, whereas [`exp_physics.py`](experiments/exp_physics.py) uses $\epsilon=0.01$. The same experiment calls any selected policy with true $\Delta>0$ a “violation,” although a certificate with positive $\epsilon$ promises only $\Delta\leq\epsilon$. Its reported “power” likewise counts selection of any policy better than raw, rather than power to meet the declared useful-policy target. These definitions must be aligned before coverage, power, or resource conclusions can be drawn. The paper's own limitations section notes that the cluster task has virtually identical risks for its even and raw targets; it therefore does not independently confirm the sign-reversal mechanism.

### 6. The remaining contribution is not sufficiently novel or consequential

For independent equal-weight orbit draws, the expression

$$
R_S(a)=\mathbb{E}[(\bar\mu_a(X)-Y)^2]+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}_g\mu_g(X)]}{k}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}_g\sigma_g^2(X)]}{S}
$$

is a conditional bias–variance decomposition. The calibration split $\operatorname{Var}(D)=\operatorname{Var}_X(\mathbb{E}[D\mid X])+\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{Var}(D\mid X)]$ is the law of total variance. They are helpful for defining the correct deployed-risk estimand and explaining simulation results, but the manuscript has no lower bound, instance-dependent allocation theorem, or matched-resource advantage over generic paired risk calibration. It has also withdrawn the claim that the observed preparation-versus-shot frontier reflects an allocation rule rather than a loose inequality, while Section 6 continues to call certification “preparation-limited at fixed total budget.” These statements need reconciliation.

The paper correctly distinguishes its estimand from Catak's finite-shot generalization problem and Chen's symmetry-release decision. That distinction establishes a question; it does not by itself establish a top-tier result. Chen already includes independent validation and scalar-probe access, so the comparison should use the closest applicable procedure under equal access. The original research brief specifically required a measurable resource separation or a consequential new regime. Neither is shown.

## Presentation and reproducibility

The manuscript's candid limitations and the release of code and generated results are valuable. They make the above problems possible to diagnose. Several presentation changes would also help a revised study:

- Label the first risk term as an integrated squared discrepancy rather than an unqualified “bias squared,” and state which equal-weight, independent-draw assumptions Equation (1) requires.
- Report physical-task selection rates, actual chosen-policy risks, fallback rates, and resource counts in the manuscript, not only oracle-best risks from exact simulator expectations.
- Show full averaging, raw prediction, and the best partial mixture side by side when claiming that the *sign* of averaging's benefit flips. The current physical-task table reports only the best policy for each target.
- State which numerical outcomes were fixed before seeing final evaluation data and document any protocol amendments, especially the changed physical-task $\epsilon$.

I inspected the manuscript, generated tables, results, and implementation. I could not run the stated unit tests with the available `python3` because NumPy is absent from that interpreter. This does not affect the mathematical and accounting objections above.

## Decision

**Reject.** A new submission would need a correct simultaneous certificate, an actual calibration and deployment measurement ledger, a physically justified group action, and matched-resource comparisons with generic paired certification and applicable quantum measurement baselines. It would then need either a nontrivial theoretical separation or a reproducible operational advantage on two genuinely distinct task mechanisms. The present work is a promising control study, but its central certification and resource claims are not established.
